The bug that was in the data, not the model

The first backtest made the model look no better than the rule of thumb. A handful of forecasts were off by 50–90%, all starting on the same dates in different buildings. The cause: four buildings' meters drop to near zero at the same hour and recover days later, a shared data-logger failure. The model trusted the broken readings and forecast an empty building.

Raw meter readingAfter outage repair

Fix: a reading below 5% of the building's trailing 4-week median is flagged as an outage, repaired from the same hour a week earlier before it reaches the model, excluded from training, and never used to score a forecast.

What it means for a facility manager

Peak demand sets demand charges, so finding each day's peak matters as much as average accuracy.

Honest reading: pinning the exact peak hour is hard, and the model is only modestly better than the baseline at it, because these buildings run on a flat plateau through the working day: on about half of the days, an hour more than an hour away from the peak is within 3% of it. Its clearer advantage is the size of the peak and the total energy, which is what purchasing and demand planning depend on.

What the error is worth scenario

If energy is bought ahead on the forecast, each kWh of error is settled at a worse price. The kWh of error are measured in the backtest; the price per kWh of error is an assumption, shown as a range.

What drives the forecast

Mean absolute SHAP value per feature, for the weighted ensemble. The model predicts consumption relative to each building's previous-week average, so effects read as a share of a typical hour. That is also why one model serves buildings whose sizes differ by 100×.

How it was tested

  1. Real data. 10 buildings at one Washington, DC site from the Building Data Genome Project 2 (hourly meters, 2016–2017), with real hourly weather from Open-Meteo.
  2. Model. A weighted ensemble of XGBoost, LightGBM and Random Forest, trained on the earlier data with lag, rolling, calendar and weather features.
  3. Rolling-origin backtest. recursive 48-hour forecasts from start dates spread across the held-out autumn–winter 2017 period. No forecast sees the future.
  4. Honest uncertainty. 95% intervals by bootstrapping whole forecasts (hours inside one forecast are not independent).
  5. Reproducible. make collect-real preprocess train backtest report regenerates every number on this page.
